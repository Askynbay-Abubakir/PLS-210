What is the relationship between average regional income per capita and voter turnout rates across Kazakhstan's 20 subnational units in the 23 August 2026 Kurultai election?

Illustrative only — shows the hypothesized direction, not real data.

Epistemological positioning

I think my project fits the positivist tradition because I am trying to test an empirical relationship between two measurable variables: average regional income per capita and voter turnout. I have a hypothesis about the expected direction of this relationship, and I want to use observable data from Kazakhstan's regions to see whether the predicted relationship exists. My goal is not to understand how people interpret voting or what voting means to them, which would fit more with an interpretivist approach. It is also more than simply describing regional turnout because I am using a theory to develop an expectation that can be empirically tested.

Scope of the claim: because my data are aggregate regional data, my conclusions will be about the relationship between regional income and regional turnout, not individual voters. I can say whether a wealthier region had a higher turnout rate than a poorer region — I cannot say how any specific individual behaved.

Contribution

This study contributes to the literature by examining whether differences in average income are associated with differences in voter turnout across Kazakhstan's 20 subnational units in the 23 August 2026 Kurultai election. While existing research has largely examined the relationship between socioeconomic resources and political participation at the individual level and across countries, this study applies a regional-level perspective to Kazakhstan. It therefore provides evidence on whether socioeconomic differences between Kazakhstan's regions correspond to differences in electoral participation, while recognizing that aggregate data cannot establish individual-level behavior or the specific mechanism behind the relationship.

Literature review

Existing research generally suggests that socioeconomic conditions can influence political participation, but the relationship is not necessarily simple or universal. Brady, Verba, and Schlozman (1995) provide a theoretical explanation, arguing that people with higher socioeconomic status have greater access to resources such as money, time, and civic skills, which make political participation easier. Cross-national research by Huijsmans et al. (2022) and Matsubayashi and Sakaiya (2021) provides empirical evidence that higher-income individuals are generally more likely to vote, while also showing that this relationship can vary depending on the broader economic context, particularly levels of inequality. Carreras and Castañeda-Angarita (2019) further complicate this assumption by showing that economic conditions can affect turnout differently across socioeconomic groups, suggesting that higher income does not automatically produce higher participation. However, these four studies primarily examine individual-level data and do not specifically examine Kazakhstan. Zholdybalina, Yermakhanova, and Akhantayeva (2026) address part of this geographical gap by showing, using a nationally representative survey of Kazakhstan, that factors such as education, age, and settlement type are associated with voting, but they do not examine income or regional differences in turnout.

Therefore, the remaining gap is whether regional differences in income are associated with differences in voter turnout within Kazakhstan, particularly in the context of the August 2026 Kurultai elections. My study addresses this gap by shifting the focus from individual-level socioeconomic characteristics to regional-level income and turnout in Kazakhstan.

Theory and hypotheses

My theory is that voting involves costs, such as the time and money required to reach and participate at a polling station. People with greater economic resources may face fewer constraints in bearing these costs, making political participation relatively easier. At the regional level, this suggests that people living in higher-income regions may have fewer resource-related barriers to voting, which could be associated with higher voter turnout.

H1: Regions in Kazakhstan with higher average income per capita will have higher voter turnout rates in the 23 August 2026 Kurultai election.
IV — average regional income per capita DV — regional voter turnout rate

One rival explanation is infrastructure and access to polling stations: wealthier regions may have better transportation or easier access to polling stations, which could increase turnout independently of individuals' personal resources. My current design cannot fully distinguish between these explanations, so I treat the income–turnout relationship as an association rather than proof of the proposed mechanism.
